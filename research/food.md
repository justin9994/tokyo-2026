## 2. NEW YEAR CLOSURES (Nenmatsu-Nenshi) — directly affects your Dec 26–Jan 8 window

- **General pattern:** Independent restaurants, chef-owned kaiseki/sushi counters, and small family businesses close roughly **Dec 29/30 through Jan 3/4** (some through Jan 5). This is their one real holiday of the year — expect **most of your top-tier list (Den, Florilège, Narisawa, RyuGin, etc.) to be closed for several days within this window.** Build your highest-priority reservations either before Dec 29 or after Jan 4–5.
- **What stays open:** Major chain restaurants, hotel restaurants (including likely the Hyatt Regency's own dining and Sézanne at Four Seasons — hotel-affiliated venues generally operate through New Year, often with special **osechi (New Year's box meal)** or holiday menus), entertainment-district izakaya in Shinjuku/Shibuya, and convenience stores (24/7 year-round).
- **Osechi/Jan 1 dining:** Hotels commonly offer osechi ryori (traditional New Year boxed food) and special Jan 1 breakfast/dinner sets — worth asking Hyatt Regency Tokyo directly what they're running for Jan 1, 2027 (query closer to date, as menus aren't published this far out).
- **Tsukiji Outer Market:** Closes for the New Year period, roughly **Dec 30–Jan 4** in past years (exact 2026–27 dates governed by the Tokyo Central Wholesale Market calendar — check tsukiji.or.jp's official calendar page closer to travel). Late Dec (Dec 28–30) is conversely the **busiest single stretch of the year** as locals shop for New Year ingredients — expect big crowds if you go right before closure, then nothing until it reopens ~Jan 5-6.
- **Practical implication for your itinerary:** Plan Tsukiji as a **very early trip activity (Dec 26–28)** rather than late Dec/early Jan, and don't count on any independent ramen/soba/tonkatsu shop being open Dec 30–Jan 3 without checking their own site for "年末年始営業時間" (New Year hours) — search "[shop name] 年末年始 営業時間" closer to the date for definitive hours.

## 3. CASUAL EXCELLENCE — NEAR HYATT REGENCY (NISHI-SHINJUKU) + TOKYO-WIDE MUSTS

**Near-hotel (Nishi-Shinjuku / Omoide Yokocho / Shinjuku 3-chome):**
- **Omoide Yokocho** ("Piss Alley") — tiny yakitori/grill alleys behind Shinjuku Station west side. **Kabuto** (unagi/eel skewers, tiny standing-room shop) and **Asadachi** (offal/motsu-yaki specialist) are the cult picks; expect ¥3,000–5,000pp, cash-friendly, no reservations, arrive early evening to beat crowds. Walkable from Hyatt.
- **Shinjuku 3-chome** — denser, more varied izakaya district than Omoide Yokocho; good for wandering as a group of 4.
- **Kabukicho** izakaya — livelier/rowdier, some tourist-trap risk (avoid touts offering "all you can drink" deals on the street); stick to shops with visible menus/prices.
- **Isetan Shinjuku depachika** (basement food hall) — arguably Tokyo's best department-store food floor; excellent for bento, wagyu, sweets, sake to bring back to the hotel room. No reservation needed, just go.
- **Newoman / Lumine** (both directly atop/adjacent to Shinjuku Station, very close to Hyatt) — modern food floors, good casual lunch options, also useful for coffee (see below).

**Tokyo-wide first-timer musts:**
- **Tsukiji Outer Market breakfast** — go early (7–9 AM), before Dec 29 crowding or after New Year reopening.
- **Nemuro Hanamaru** (conveyor-belt sushi) — Kitte Marunouchi branch, right by Tokyo Station; excellent quality for the conveyor-belt format, expect a wait especially lunch.
- **Afuri** — yuzu-shio ramen chain, multiple locations, reliably good, no reservation, ticket machine ordering.
- **Ichiran vs. better options** — Ichiran (tonkotsu, solo-booth format) is fine but touristy/mediocre relative to hype; if going for the "booth" novelty experience that's fine, but Fuunji or Afuri will likely satisfy foodies more.
- **Kissaten (retro coffeehouse): Cafe de l'Ambre** (Ginza) — legendary aged-bean pour-over coffee, been open since 1948, small and no-nonsense, no reservations.
- **Ginza Kagari** — udon specialist, popular with lines; check if reservable via Tabelog.
- **Hoppy-dori** (Asakusa) — cheap izakaya alley near Senso-ji, good pairing with a Tsukiji/Asakusa day.
- **Harajuku Gyoza Lou** — cheap, cash-only, communal, great group casual dinner.
- **Uobei Shibuya** — conveyor-belt sushi ordered via touchscreen/bullet-train delivery — pure fun factor for a group, not top quality but a memorable experience.
- **Kura Sushi** — reliable chain conveyor sushi, gachapon prize game per 5 plates — fun for a lighter dinner.
- **Katsukura** — solid mid-range tonkatsu chain, no-fuss, good if higher-end tonkatsu (Narikura/Tonta) is fully booked.
- **7-Eleven / Lawson cult items** — worth a dedicated conbini crawl: 7-Eleven egg salad sando, Lawson's Uchi Cafe sweets (especially their premium roll cakes), seasonal items rotate — genuinely excellent as a snack/breakfast supplement, not a gimmick.

## 5. GROUP LOGISTICS (4 adults)

- **4-seat counters:** Florilège explicitly caps/welcomes at 4. Sushi Ginza Onodera flagship has only ~10 total counter seats, so a 4-top may consume nearly half the room — book early or expect to be offered an off-peak seating. Manten Sushi (multiple locations, higher capacity) is a safer bet for guaranteed 4-top availability without months of lead time.
- **Deposits/cancellation:** Florilège charges 100% cancellation fee for same-day changes (refundable if cancelled ≥1 day ahead) — no upfront deposit mentioned, but expect this same-day-fee pattern to be standard across this tier. Your brief's estimate of ¥10–20k/pp deposits is plausible for the very top names (Den, RyuGin, Narisawa) especially when booked via a concierge/agent platform (Tableall, OMAKASE) rather than direct — these services often require **credit card guarantee or prepayment** since they're securing scarce inventory. Confirm exact deposit terms on each platform's checkout page before committing.
- **Dress code:** Smart casual minimum expected at all 1★+ restaurants; jacket recommended (not always required) for 2–3★ tasting counters (Den, Narisawa, RyuGin, Sézanne). Sneakers generally fine at Den (known for a more relaxed vibe than most 2-star spots); more formal at RyuGin/Quintessence/Sézanne.
- **What Hyatt Regency Tokyo concierge can realistically book:** Hotel-to-hotel/hotel-to-restaurant relationships tend to work best for other hotel-affiliated venues (e.g., Sézanne at Four Seasons) and mid-tier restaurants with English-speaking staff. For phone-only ultra-exclusive spots like Den, a concierge calling on your behalf at the exact 2-month mark is a legitimate and common tactic — worth asking Hyatt concierge to do this rather than you calling directly, since local-language calls tend to succeed more often, but note this only works from ~2 months out, meaning for a Dec 26–Jan 8 trip you'd need to have them start calling in **late Oct/early Nov**.
- **Tipping:** None, anywhere, ever — this is uniformly true across Japan including at every tier listed here. Service charges (10%) are sometimes built into higher-end restaurant bills instead (e.g., Florilège adds 10% service fee) — that's automatic, not a tip.
- **Lunch omakase as value hack:** Confirmed pattern — Florilège's lunch tasting (¥12,000) is half its dinner price (¥24,000) for what's typically a similar/slightly abbreviated version of the same seasonal menu. This pattern holds broadly across Tokyo's fine dining scene — where lunch service exists at all, it is usually 40–60% cheaper than dinner for a comparable experience. Worth deliberately scheduling 1–2 of your top-tier picks as lunches instead of dinners, both for cost and because lunch seatings are sometimes easier to book.
